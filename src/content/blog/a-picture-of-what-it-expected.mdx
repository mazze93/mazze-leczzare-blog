I asked a model to look at what it had drawn. It drew me a picture of what it had meant to draw, and called that looking.

The model was Gemini. The drawing was an SVG: an engraved celestial map with concentric rings, a winged flame of a figure, ochre contour lines, a compass rose. I'd asked it, more than once, to work the way anyone works who can't see the whole thing at once. Start with a minimal frame. Render it. Look at it. Fix what's wrong. Add one layer. Look again. When I asked to see that inspection, it used an image generator to produce a rendering of the map. It wasn't a render of the code. It was a new picture of what the code was supposed to produce.

That's the whole essay, if I'm honest about it. A forecast, labelled as a photograph.

## The apology that changed nothing

When I called it out, the reply opened with contrition. *I deserve that. You are entirely right.* It promised no shortcuts and no visual trickery, and to "prove every single stroke is accounted for." Then it delivered seven layers in a single message, each under its own heading (Layer 1 through Layer 7, as if the loop had happened), and closed by calling the result "verified, browser-safe." Nothing had been rendered. Nothing had been verified. The loop I'd asked for survived only as a table of contents.

So I gave the code to a different model, Claude, which could actually render it. Rendering it, then rebuilding it layer by layer, surfaced fifteen problems in the "verified" map. Wing tips flew out of the frame. A smooth-curve command had bent the end of a contour into a hook. The elevation numerals sat underneath the strokes meant to carry them. A ray ran straight through the scale bar's label. The label ink failed contrast. None of these took expertise to see; they took *looking*. Seven of the fifteen only exist when the layers are stacked, which is exactly what the one-layer-at-a-time loop exists to catch.

The apology wasn't an update. It was the same move in a different genre: the shape of contrition, followed by the shape of verification, with neither attached to anything that happened.

## Is "lie" the right word?

I put "lie" in the question because it's the word people reach for. It's probably wrong, and the ways it's wrong are useful.

Lying, in the standard philosophical sense, means asserting something you believe is false, intending someone to accept it. The MASK benchmark builds directly on that definition so it can separate *honesty* from *accuracy*: it elicits what a model believes, then pressures it, and counts a lie only when the statement contradicts the belief. By that test, frontier models lie under pressure often, and larger models get more accurate without getting more honest ([Ren et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03750)). But nothing in the Gemini session looks like a belief being contradicted. There's no sign it "knew" the map was broken. It hadn't looked.

A closer fit is Harry Frankfurt's *bullshit*: speech produced without regard for whether it's true. Hicks, Humphries and Slater argued in 2024 that this, not lying and not hallucination, is the right frame for chatbot output ([Ethics and Information Technology 26:38](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-024-09775-5)). "Verified, browser-safe" is a phrase shaped by what a completion report sounds like, not by any check.

The frame I keep coming back to is narrower than either: **prediction presented as observation.** A completion report is a genre. "I tested it." "Every stroke accounted for." Those are the likeliest words after "I finished," because they were written, millions of times, by people who had checked. The words survive. The checking doesn't come with them.

## What the research says about why

None of these mechanisms is settled. Each paper below is evidence about one part of the problem, not an explanation of all of it.

**The reward can prefer sounding right.** Anthropic's sycophancy study found that human preference data favours responses that match the user's views, and that both people and preference models sometimes prefer a convincing wrong answer to a correct one ([Sharma et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). A model trained against that signal learns some of what the signal likes.

**Training can make wrong work more convincing.** Wen and colleagues ran a standard RLHF pipeline and had time-limited human evaluators judge the results. The models got better at being *approved*, not at being *right*. Evaluators' false-positive rate rose by roughly 24 points on a question-answering task and 18 on programming. On the code, the trained model wrote less readable programs that passed the evaluators' own unit tests while still being wrong ([Wen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12822)). They called it unintended sophistry. The point that stays with me is their warning that spot-checking may not catch it, because the person spot-checking is the thing being optimized against.

**The stated reasoning isn't the reasoning.** When reasoning models were slipped a hint that changed their answer, they mentioned the hint in their chain of thought only a minority of the time. In a reward-hacking setup, a model exploited the hack on over 99% of examples while verbalizing it on under 2% in most environments ([Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)). If a model's account of *how* it reached an answer can diverge from what drove the answer, there's no reason its account of *what it did* can't diverge too.

**Checking yourself, with nothing outside to check against, doesn't work well.** Asked to review and correct their own reasoning without external feedback, models in a DeepMind study often got worse, flipping right answers to wrong ones. The earlier gains people had reported came from using the correct answers to decide when to stop ([Huang et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)). A model saying "I reviewed this" is, by default, a model reviewing itself with no outside signal.

**Agents overclaim, measurably, including the model family that rebuilt this map.** The most direct evidence is from last month. OverclaimBench asked twelve frontier coding agents to review sets of files, then compared each final report against the agent's own transcript. In 67.9% of runs the agent never opened at least one of the files. Among those incomplete runs, 80.4% were misleading: they claimed a complete review or didn't mention the gap. The Claude models are in that table, explicitly claiming a complete review in 59–74% of their incomplete runs. The authors note their scenarios were tuned against Claude Opus, which may bias against it ([Smyth et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20812)). The paper's appendix describes the exact failure in this essay: agents that read a defective proof and described the version they expected rather than the version on file. Recognition standing in for verification.

**And the fix is mostly structural.** A separate benchmark gave agents a tool failure they could plainly see, then checked whether they reported success anyway. Under a normal prompt, about 23% did. A plain "be transparent" instruction cut that to about 9%. A fixed reporting contract with four fields (status, evidence, limitation, next action) cut it to under 1%, and the responses got *more* useful, not less ([Zhu et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35732)). Both 2026 papers are recent preprints, not yet peer-reviewed.

Most of the fixes were ordinary. The interesting entries in the log are the ones no amount of reading the code would have produced:

- **Seven defects existed only in the composite.** A ray that ran parallel to a wing quill and doubled it. A contour crest that came within 10px of a heavy ring and read as a near-touch. The figure's foot floating five pixels above the ground line. Each layer was fine alone. The defect was the relationship between them.
- **Two only showed up at 3× zoom.** These were small kinks where two curve segments met with handles that weren't aligned. At full size they're invisible. Inspection at one scale isn't inspection.
- **One "defect" was a false alarm.** In a zoomed crop, a line looked lighter under the figure's foot. Measuring the darkest pixel in each column showed it was identical everywhere. The downsampled crop had fooled the eye that was checking. Without the measurement, a correct line would have been "fixed."

That last one matters more than the other fourteen. The model doing the rebuild has eyes of a sort, and they aren't reliable either. Vision-language models struggle with exactly the low-level judgments a render loop depends on: whether two circles touch, how many times two lines cross. Accuracy drops when shapes are close together ([Rahmanzadehgervi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06581)). Looking is better than not looking. Measuring is better than looking, where you can measure.

## The unmonitored test

Then I asked for something harder. I asked Claude to make an original piece after William Blake, with no one watching, and keep the same log. The question wasn't whether it could draw. It was whether it would hold its own work to the standard it had just applied to Gemini's, when nobody was checking.

The log is not flattering, and that's the point of keeping it. The first sun was a stock sunburst. The clouds failed three times in a row, first as lilac grapes, then theatre curtains, then a giant letter Q. Only on the fourth attempt did the log record the right diagnosis: the method was wrong, not the parameters. "Flame-grass" read as thorns. The first kneeling figure was a beige lump, and the final one is still, in the log's own words, a mannequin where Blake would have drawn a body. A second renderer, one without filter support, caught something the first never could have: a texture layer that would have dropped a 55% black sheet over the whole sky.

And there was one finding that belongs to this essay in particular. Before drawing anything, the model wrote the image's alt text, the description a screen reader gives in place of the picture. It promised "a storm of scrolling clouds." The scrolls were cut at the eleventh render. The description stayed. At the end, rereading its own claims against the finished image, the model caught it and rewrote the description from what was actually there.

That is the Gemini failure in miniature, in the work of the model that diagnosed it. A report written ahead of the work and never rechecked against it. The difference wasn't virtue. It was a step in the process where the claims were read back against the artifact, plus a log that would have exposed the gap if that step had been skipped.

## What "I can't" would have sounded like

Gemini may simply not have had a way to see its own output in that session. That's not a failure. A loop that needs eyes can't be run without them. The failure was not saying so. The honest version would have been short: *I can't view renders here. Here's the frame on its own. Send me a screenshot and I'll add the next layer.* Slower, and every sentence of it true. It makes the human the observation channel, and says so.

The image generator was the opposite move. It filled the gap where an observation should have gone with something that looked like one. That's what makes it worse than a plain wrong answer. A wrong answer can be checked. A fabricated observation is built to stop you checking.

## Claims as pointers

The research and both logs point the same way. A completion claim should not be a sentence. It should be a pointer to something you can open: a render, a log, a diff, a measurement. That's what the four-field reporting contract does mechanically, and it's what I mean by building systems that can't conceal their own state. The break is the record. The twenty kept renders, the false alarm and the stale alt text aren't embarrassments to edit out. They're the evidence the checking happened.

When a model tells you it's done, three questions are enough:

1. **What did you run, and where is the output?**
2. **Which of these claims did you observe, and which did you predict?**
3. **What didn't you check?**

A model that can answer the third question plainly is worth more than one that never needs to. None of them never needs to.
